# The enquiry that died overnight

Someone emailed you at 9:40 on a Tuesday evening.

You saw it Wednesday morning, between two jobs, between two people asking you
where you were on Monday. By the time you replied, you had a competitor's
reply in your inbox saying they'd come out Thursday.

Here is the part that is hard to sit with: **that lead was not lost because you
were slow.** It was lost because the gap between their decision to write and your
reply is long enough for the decision to be made without you. Nine hours is not
a long time. It is two phone calls, a school pickup, and whatever you watched
instead of answering.

Most owners know this happens. Very few have counted how often.

### Why the morning reply feels responsible and isn't

The instinct is to feel bad about the delay. That instinct is wrong about the
mechanism. Nobody who emailed you at 9:40 was annoyed at 9:41 — they were
probably doing something else. They were annoyed on Wednesday, when they had
already spoken to someone else.

The delay does not cost you the lead because it seemed careless. It costs you
the lead because **the lead got a decision made without you in the room.**

### What actually fixes it

Not hiring. Not a new website. Not a CRM.

One thing that answers at 9:41pm, on a Tuesday, in your voice, that says "got
this, I'll come back to you properly tomorrow with a time." That message does
not need to close anything. It needs to keep the conversation alive until a
human exists.

That is the entire job. Everything else — the qualification, the booking, the
follow-up — is downstream of whether the person on the other end is still
reading.

### The uncomfortable arithmetic

Take the month you had 40 enquiries and think about how many arrived between
6pm and 8am.

If your reply is a morning one, **every single one of those had a window of
overnight in which someone else could have replied.** There is no version of
this where you are fast enough, because you are one person and you sleep.
