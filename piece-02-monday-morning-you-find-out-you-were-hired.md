# Monday morning, you find out you were hired

The worst moment in this business is not the lost enquiry. The lost enquiry
doesn't tell you.

The lost *job* tells you. Someone books a call, you quote, they go quiet, and
three weeks later you see their van in a different driveway, or you drive past a
sign with a name you haven't seen before.

Now work out what they told you.

They didn't say "we went with someone cheaper." They almost certainly didn't say
"you were slow." What they said, if you ask — and almost nobody asks — is
something closer to:

> "You quoted three days and we needed it next week."

or

> "We heard back from someone else in the afternoon."

That second one is the one worth staring at. **You didn't lose on price. You lost
on elapsed time**, and elapsed time is the one variable you thought was a solved
problem because you personally answer everything.

### The three questions to ask a lost job

Ask three of these. Not to win the job back — to find out which one you keep
hearing.

1. **"What was the deciding factor?"** The answer is almost never price. It is
   usually availability, or trust, or whether they had to chase you.
2. **"What did our last reply look like from your side?"** This one stings and
   is the most valuable.
3. **"Did you get other quotes?"** If yes, you were in a race you didn't know
   you were running, against a response time you'd have called generous on
   Monday.

### Why you cannot fix this by being better

You can be excellent. Your work can be the best they have seen. It does not
matter, if the quote lands at 4pm Thursday for a job they needed Monday.

**Quality is a tiebreaker and response time is the entry ticket.** Most small
service businesses are competing on entry ticket and then congratulating
themselves on quality.

### What to change

One acknowledgement, sent immediately, that says you have it and when you'll
speak. Not a proposal. Not a quote. A receipt.

The quote can take you a day. The receipt should take zero.
