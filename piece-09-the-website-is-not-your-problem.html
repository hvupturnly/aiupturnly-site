# Your website is not your problem

You have been meaning to fix the website for eight months.

New logo, cleaner photos, maybe a booking button, definitely a testimonial

slider. The current version looks dated and you know it every time you send a

customer the link, which you therefore don't.

**Here is the thing: fixing the website will not change how many enquiries you

get, and you can verify that claim for free this week.**

Do the experiment

Write down every enquiry you received in the last 30 days and where it came

from.

If the answer is "I don't know" or "a lot of them were phone calls" — which is

the usual answer — then you have no evidence that the website is the problem.

You have a feeling that it is, and feelings are expensive when they drive

a 5,000-dollar rebuild.

What the website actually does

For a 5–50 person service business, the website has exactly two jobs:

1. Say what you do and where you work, so a stranger can go from search to

confident in under a minute.

2. Hand off to an enquiry. Phone, form, email.

That's it. It is not a sales page, not a portfolio, not a statement of values.

The owners who win with content are not the ones with better sites — they are

the ones whose enquiry path works, so that traffic converts once it arrives.

Why the rebuild feels productive

It is visible. It has a finish line. The enquiry path has none of those

properties: it is invisible, it is continuous, and nobody can photograph it.

So we rebuild the site, feel like progress was made, and the phone still rings

at the same rate — while the thing that would actually help sits unfixed for

another eight months.

Do this instead

Spend one evening on the only question that matters: **when a stranger arrives,

what happens, and how long until a human replies?** Fix that. Then fix the

website if you still want to, and enjoy it, because at that point it will be a

vanity project rather than a bottleneck.

That is a much better use of an owner's scarcest resource.
